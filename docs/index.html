Dimples on the Rhine

From a long wooden boat on the Rhine, a friend watched small dimples drift across the water, some of them lasting a surprisingly long time. Each is the top of a whirlpool. Below, built up from scratch: why whirlpools come in pairs, why the river's last so long, and why their dance is Hamiltonian.

Click the water to drop a whirlpool · shift-click spins it the other way

1A dimple is the top of a whirlpool

Water going round in a circle needs a push toward the centre, and the only thing inside water that can push is pressure. So the pressure must drop toward the middle of a whirlpool. At the surface the pressure is pinned by the air above, and the only way to lower it underneath is to have less water on top: the surface sags into a small funnel, a millimetre or two deep.

That funnel is a weak lens. It spreads the sunlight passing through its middle and gathers it toward the rim, so the riverbed shows a dark disk with a bright edge, a caustic. The sun isn't straight overhead, so the shadow lands a little to one side of its dimple. In the simulation above, each dimple is a small glassy lens (inside the ring marker), and its shadow sits just below and to the left.

For a whirlpool of circulation Γ with a core of radius a, balancing pressure against swirl gives a dip η = −Γ² / 8π²g(r² + a²): about 1.5 mm at the centre for Γ = 60 cm²/s and a = 6 mm.

2Measuring spin: walk around a loop

How much does a whirlpool spin? Walk around a loop and add up how hard the water pushes you along the way. That total is the circulation, Γ.

Any loop that goes around the whirlpool's core gives nearly the same answer, however big or lopsided, and a loop that misses the core gives almost nothing. The spin is concentrated in the core: outside it the water swirls, but a tiny paddle wheel carried along there hardly turns. Add a second whirlpool spinning the other way, and a loop around both gives zero.

3Spin lives on lines

From above, a whirlpool looks like a point. But the water is three-dimensional, and what spins is a line, the whirlpool's axis, carrying on down into the water. Thicken the line into a tube: its circulation is the same at every depth. The dimple is just where the line meets the surface. A line (one dimension) and a plane (two) in space (three) meet in 1 + 2 − 3 = 0 dimensions, a point. That's why the surface shows whirlpools as points.

4A vortex line can't just stop

Helmholtz noticed in 1858 that vortex lines behave like the field lines of a magnet: they cannot end in the middle of the water. The argument takes a single loop. Suppose a tube of spin did stop somewhere. Put a loop around it: circulation Γ. Slide the loop along the tube and past its end. The circulation around a loop can only change when the loop sweeps across spin, and no spin crosses the side of a tube, so it is still Γ. But now the loop surrounds water that isn't spinning at all, and it can be shrunk to a point, where the circulation is plainly zero. So Γ = 0, a contradiction. (Press Ends in the water? above.)

A vortex line therefore has three options: close into a ring, like a smoke ring; end on the riverbed; or end on the surface. The last two are the ones we see.

5Why whirlpools come in pairs

Take an arch, a tube with both feet on the surface. Follow the spin along it, down one leg and up the other. The same spin looks counterclockwise from above at one foot and clockwise at the other. One arch, two dimples, opposite spins.

Kelvin found a second reason in 1869. In water without friction, the circulation around any loop that moves with the water never changes. Draw a big loop in still water: its circulation is zero, and it stays zero while you stir inside it, as long as the loop never touches the paddle. So whatever spin a paddle makes, it makes equal amounts both ways. Drag a paddle through a pool and you see exactly this: two dimples, spinning opposite ways, sailing off together. Each is carried along by the other's swirl, so the pair moves in a straight line, crosswise to the line joining them. Press Drop a pair in the simulation, and watch the total spin readout: pairs add nothing to it.

6Where the Rhine's dimples come from

The dimples that started this page were almost certainly not made by paddles: there were hardly any boats about, and they showed up beside and ahead of the boat more than behind it. The river makes them itself.

Kelvin's rule has one loophole, friction, and the river's friction is at its bed. Water touching the bed is held still, while a hand's width above it the current is racing. That shear is spin: a paddle wheel lying on the bed would turn. So the bed is where the river's spin is born, as a sheet of vortex lines lying across the current. The sheet is unstable. It wrinkles into arches, hairpin vortices, whose heads lift into faster water and get dragged up and downstream.

When an arch reaches the surface, the surface cuts through it and its two legs become a pair of dimples with opposite spins. A leg can also keep its other end on the bed, and then you see a single dimple, which Helmholtz allows. The rising water spreads out at the surface as a smooth, glassy patch that river people call a boil, and dimples gather at its edges, where surface water converges and sinks. Bridge piers shed whirlpools from alternate sides, and the shear between the main current and slow water along a bank or behind a groyne makes rows of whirlpools that all spin the same way.

The pattern seen from the boat makes sense too. A dimple is only a millimetre or two deep, about as steep as a small wind ripple, so ripples hide it: you need smooth water. Behind the boat the propeller's wash churns the surface; ahead and beside it, the river's own dimples float on undisturbed water.

7Why they live so long: the surface makes the flow flat

The surface does two things to a whirlpool. First, it stands it upright. Air is far too thin to drag on water, so right at the surface there is no sideways pull, and that forces vortex lines to meet the surface at right angles: sideways spin can't survive there, only upright whirlpools can. Second, water near the surface can't move up through it, so the flow there is nearly flat, two-dimensional.

That matters because of stretching. Turbulence in three dimensions runs on stretched vortex tubes: a stretched tube gets thinner and spins faster, like a skater pulling in their arms. (Its circulation can't change, so as its cross-section shrinks its spin must grow.) Stretch, spin up, break into smaller eddies, and within seconds friction eats the smallest ones. In two dimensions there is no third direction to stretch into, and each drop of water simply keeps its spin as it is carried along.

So a surface whirlpool can only fade by friction slowly smearing out its core. Water's own friction widens the core as a² → a² + 4νt, with ν = 0.01 cm²/s, and the dip goes like 1/a². A dimple with a 6 mm core fades to a quarter of its depth in about half a minute; one with a 2 cm core takes about five minutes. In the real river, turbulence from below frays them sooner, one more reason the long-lived ones are seen on smooth water. Flat flow has another quirk: whirlpools spinning the same way tend to merge into bigger ones instead of shattering into small ones.

8The river surface is a phase space

Flatness pays off once more. Surface water can't be squashed, so every patch keeps its area as it flows, and in the plane, keeping area is exactly what mathematicians call symplectic (it is the camel of episode 1). As a result the whole flow is described by one landscape, the stream function ψ: water moves along ψ's contour lines, like a hiker keeping to one height, and faster where the contours crowd.

dx/dt = ∂ψ/∂y,    dy/dt = −∂ψ/∂x

These are Hamilton's equations with x as position and y as momentum: the river's surface is its own phase space. Phase spaces come as position–momentum pairs, so they always have an even number of dimensions; a plane qualifies, and the three-dimensional inside of a river doesn't. Toggle Stream function above to see the landscape, then drop the Camel dye: it is stretched into filaments and its outline grows longer and longer, but its area stays at 100%.

9The whirlpools obey it too

Kirchhoff showed in 1876 that the whirlpools themselves obey Hamilton's equations. Each is carried by the others' swirl, and whirlpool k's x and y are a position–momentum pair, weighted by its spin:

Γk dxk/dt = ∂H/∂yk,   Γk dyk/dt = −∂H/∂xk,   H = −(1/4π) Σ ΓjΓk log|zj − zk|²

So N whirlpools make a 2N-dimensional phase space. The energy H, the centre of spin (two numbers) and the angular impulse are conserved; the readout under the simulation tracks them. That is just enough to keep three whirlpools regular (press Three) and not enough for four (press Four): their dance is chaotic, and nudging one by a micron changes the whole pattern within seconds.
