Dimples on the Rhine

Each dimple on a river is the top of a small whirlpool, and its shadow on the riverbed is a dark spot inside a bright caustic ring. Drop some on the water and watch how they carry each other along.

Click the water to drop a whirlpool

What a dimple is

Fast-spinning water has low pressure, so the surface above a whirlpool sags into a funnel a millimetre or two deep. That funnel is a lens: it spreads sunlight in the middle and gathers it at the rim, so the riverbed shows a dark spot inside a bright ring, a caustic.

An oar stroke leaves a half ring of spinning water; its two ends are a pair of dimples that spin opposite ways and sail off together.

The river is a phase space

Surface water can't be squashed, so every patch keeps its area, which in two dimensions is what symplectic means. Water flows along the contour lines of the stream function ψ, faster where they crowd together:

dx/dt = ∂ψ/∂y,   dy/dt = −∂ψ/∂x

That is Hamilton's rule with x as position and y as momentum. Toggle Stream function to see the landscape.

The whirlpools obey it too

Kirchhoff (1876): each whirlpool is carried by the others' swirl, and its x and y are a position–momentum pair weighted by its spin Γ. Energy and the centre of spin are conserved; watch the readout.

Three whirlpools dance regularly; four can be chaotic; and dust among three moves chaotically. The camel dye is stretched into filaments but keeps its area.
