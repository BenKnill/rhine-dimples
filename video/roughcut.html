Narration as recorded

Cold open 0:00

From a long wooden boat on the Rhine, a friend kept noticing small dimples on the water, beside the boat and ahead of it, mostly when the water was smooth. Some lasted a surprisingly long time. Each dimple sits on top of a whirlpool. Water going round in a circle needs an inward push, and that push is pressure, lower at the centre. So above the spinning water, the surface sags into a small funnel. So what keeps a small whirlpool like that together, while everything around it keeps changing? Finding out takes us through Victorian ships, smoke rings and Jupiter, and ends in a lab in Norway, reading rivers from dimples like these.

1 · The numbers that rule natural flows 0:53

Physicists size up a flow in nature with a couple of numbers. The first came from Osborne Reynolds, in Manchester, in 1883. He let a thin streak of dye into water flowing through a glass pipe. Slowly, the streak stayed a clean line. Faster, it suddenly shivered, burst, and filled the pipe. What decides it is one ratio: how strongly the moving water carries itself along, compared with how strongly its own friction holds it back. That's the Reynolds number: speed, times size, divided by viscosity. In Reynolds' pipe, the switch came at a few thousand. A river a few metres deep, flowing at a metre or two per second, has a Reynolds number in the millions. It's thoroughly turbulent. The second number came from ships. In the eighteen-seventies, the engineer William Froude towed model hulls through a long tank at Torquay, to predict the power a real ship would need. To make the right waves, a model's speed, divided by the square root of gravity times its length, has to match the real ship. That ratio is now called the Froude number, and there's a neat way to see it: it compares the current's speed with the speed of the surface's own long waves. At a low Froude number, a long wave can still creep upstream against the current. Turn the current up past a Froude number of one, and it outruns every such wave: nothing travels upstream. A river flowing a metre and a half per second over four metres of water, for example, has a Froude number of about a quarter. Gravity wins, and the surface stays nearly level, like a lid. That doesn't make the water beneath it flat. Water can still rise and sink under a level surface. But in the ocean and the atmosphere, layering and rotation really do squash vertical motion: G. I. Taylor found that in a rapidly spinning tank, a whole column of water can move as one. So physicists often reach for a model: flow that moves only sideways, the same at every depth. It will turn out to be powerful, and incomplete.

2 · What flatness does to turbulence 3:41

In 1922, the meteorologist Lewis Fry Richardson summed up ordinary turbulence in a rhyme: big whorls have little whorls that feed on their velocity, and little whorls have lesser whorls, and so on to viscosity. Energy tumbles from large eddies to smaller ones, until friction turns it into heat. Andrei Kolmogorov made that cascade precise in 1941. Its engine is stretching. Stretch a spinning tube of fluid, and it gets thinner and spins faster, like a skater pulling in their arms. Stretch it to three times its length, and it spins three times as fast. In the sideways-only model, there's nothing to stretch: a whirl's spin is simply carried along. And the cascade changes character. Robert Kraichnan and others showed that energy now climbs to larger scales: small eddies merge into big ones, while the spin gets drawn out into ever finer filaments. You can see it in soap films, about the flattest fluid we can make, and on a planetary scale. Jupiter's weather lives in a thin, fast-spinning, layered shell. Its Great Red Spot, still wider than the Earth and in the habit of swallowing smaller storms, has an observing record going back to 1831. In the ocean, lenses of salty Mediterranean water, called meddies, spin quietly for years. So in the flat model, whirlpools last and grow. But what is a dimple, really, and why does it sit on a whirlpool at all?

3 · The rules of vortices 5:42

Back to the dimple. In a simple model of a whirlpool's core, its depth depends on two things that really vary. Call the whirlpool's strength gamma. Double it, and the dip gets four times deeper. Double the width of its core, as an ageing whirlpool slowly does, and the dip falls to a quarter. That funnel is also a lens: in shallow water, it casts a dark shadow on the bed, with a bright edge. Gamma is the circulation. Walk a loop in the water, adding up how fast the water flows along your path, step by step. Well outside the core, double the loop's radius: the water is about half as fast, but the walk is twice as long, so the total barely changes. From above, a whirlpool looks like a point. But what spins is really a line, running down into the river. A line meets the surface in a single point, and that point is the dimple. In 1858, Hermann von Helmholtz worked out the rules these vortex lines obey, and one is striking: a tube of spin can't just end inside the water. Suppose it did. Slide a loop past the end without crossing it, and its circulation can't change. Now shrink the loop to a point: zero. So the tube must close into a ring, or end on the riverbed or the surface. Helmholtz's work had an unexpected sequel. In Edinburgh, Peter Guthrie Tait built a box that puffed out smoke rings, and showed them to his friend William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin. The rings bounced off each other, wobbled, and survived their collisions. Kelvin was captivated. By Helmholtz's rules, a vortex in a perfect fluid can never be created or destroyed, so a smoke ring there would last forever. Perhaps, Kelvin thought, atoms were knotted vortex rings in the ether, each kind of knot a different element. The theory was wrong. But Tait spent years drawing up tables of knots, hoping to match them to the elements, and those tables helped start the mathematics of knot theory. Kelvin also gave the rule its cleanest form: in a perfect fluid, circulation around a loop moving with the water never changes. A big loop far from a paddle stays at zero, so the paddle's spin comes in equal amounts both ways. Bend a tube into an arch with both feet on the surface, and you see it: two dimples, spinning opposite ways. Each rides the other's swirl, so the pair sails off together. Bring them closer, and they go faster.

4 · The river's own dimples 9:08

So where do the dimples on the Rhine come from? Probably not paddles: there were hardly any boats. The likeliest source is the riverbed. Water touching it is held still, while a little way above, the current races past. That shear is spin: a paddle wheel lying on the bed would turn. River engineers have long described the results. In 1947, Gerard Matthes wrote about kolks, violent upward surges from the beds of big rivers, and the boils they raise at the surface: smooth, bulging patches of upwelling water. Near a wall, researchers came to picture the spin rolling up into arches, called hairpin vortices, lifting away from the bed in bursts. When a rising arch reaches the surface, its head doesn't simply get sliced off. Friction lets the vortex reconnect to the surface, and its two legs become a pair of dimples. Simulations since the nineteen-nineties show what those ends do. Right at the surface, they stand upright, and they can wander across it while the tube below is twisted by the turbulence. So a surface whirlpool is caught between two effects. Friction spreads its core and weakens it. Water converging and sinking beneath it stretches it and spins it back up. Laboratory experiments on a nearly flat surface have watched both: vortices strengthening during downwellings, then spreading out after them. How fast does friction work? The spreading takes a time proportional to the core's width squared, divided by the viscosity: double the width, and spreading takes four times as long, though a wider core also starts out shallower. Water at five degrees is about one and a half times as viscous as water at twenty, so friction works that much faster in winter. Seeing one is a separate question. A dimple is shallow, not much steeper than a small ripple, so wind ripples can hide a whirlpool that is still there. Behind a motorboat, its own wake roughens the water. Ahead and beside it, dimples show up best on smooth water. So what keeps a small whirlpool together? It is the end of a vortex line that can't just stop, standing upright at the surface, spun up now and then by sinking water, and fading only slowly by friction.

5 · The hidden order 12:11

There's one more surprise, and it lives in the flat model. Think of a pendulum. Plot its angle across and its momentum up, and its whole state becomes a single moving point. Draw the contours of its energy, and the point follows a contour, rather than rolling downhill. That's Hamilton's rule, the backbone of mechanics, and it has a famous consequence: a patch of starting states can stretch, but never changes its area. In the sideways-only model, water can't be squashed, so patches of it keep their area too. That's no coincidence. A speck of water follows exactly Hamilton's rule, on a landscape called the stream function. With one steady whirlpool, the speck follows a contour. When the whirlpools move, the landscape changes beneath it, but the same rule still sets its motion. In this model, the surface is the speck's phase space. In 1876, Gustav Kirchhoff showed that the whirlpools themselves obey it: each is carried by the others' swirl, and together they follow the slopes of one landscape, with each whirlpool's strength as a weight. Three whirlpools dance regularly. Four can turn chaotic: in these particular runs, started a micron apart, they part ways within seconds.

6 · Reading the river 13:54

Now put back what the flat model left out: water rising and sinking underneath. A real patch of surface water doesn't have to keep its area. It spreads where water wells up from below, and shrinks where water sinks away. Incompressibility keeps volume; only in the flat model does it keep area. And that spreading and shrinking is what a group in Trondheim, Norway, around Omer Babiker and Simen Ellingsen, has been reading from dimples. In their simulations, the number of dimples in a region tracked how strongly its surface was spreading and converging, closely, with a short delay. This year, a laboratory tank experiment, at much higher Reynolds numbers, found the same kind of link. That matters, because the same stirring renews the skin of the water, and that helps set how fast gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide cross between a river and the air. So those dimples beside the boat weren't only whirlpools that happened to last. Each was a small mark on the surface, connected to a hidden, three-dimensional flow underneath. And a friend who noticed how long some of them lasted was already, in a way, reading it.
