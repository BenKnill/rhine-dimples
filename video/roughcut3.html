Narration as recorded

Cold open 0:00

On a quiet stretch of the Rhine, we kept seeing them: small dimples on the water, drifting beside the boat. Some lasted a surprisingly long time. Each one is the top of a whirlpool. Water spinning in a circle needs something pushing it inward, and in water, that push is pressure, lower at the centre. So the surface sags into a shallow funnel. But a river is chaos. Everything in it gets sheared and torn. So how does one tiny whirlpool hold itself together?

1 · Under the dimple 0:42

Dive under one, and it's not a point at all. It's a line: a thread of spinning water running down into the river. A line meets the surface in a single point. That point is the dimple. And these lines obey a strange rule. In 1858, Hermann von Helmholtz proved that a vortex line can't just stop inside the water. Try it. Put a loop around the line and slide it off the end. The loop never crosses any spin, so its total swirl can't change. But past the end, the loop can shrink to nothing, and nothing has no swirl. So a vortex line has to close into a loop, or end on something: the riverbed, or the surface.

2 · Smoke rings 1:42

Which is exactly what a smoke ring is: a vortex line, closed into a loop. In 1867, in Edinburgh, Peter Guthrie Tait built a box that fired smoke rings, and showed it to his friend William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin. The rings bounced off each other, wobbled, and survived their collisions. Helmholtz had even predicted that two rings travelling together would take turns passing through each other. Kelvin was captivated. By Helmholtz's rules, a vortex in a perfect fluid could never be created or destroyed. So maybe atoms were smoke rings in the ether, and each element a different knot. He was wrong about atoms. But Tait spent years cataloguing knots to test the idea, and helped start the mathematics of knot theory. And a smoke ring explains the Rhine. Push a ring up into the surface, and since it can't end in the water, it joins onto the surface instead. The ring opens into an arch with two ends: two dimples, spinning opposite ways. That's why dimples so often come in pairs. Each rides the other's swirl, and the pair sails off together.

3 · The river's own smoke rings 3:30

But there are no smoke boxes on the Rhine, and hardly any paddles. So what's making them? The riverbed. Water touching the bottom is held still, while the current races past just above. That shear rolls up into arches, like half smoke rings, lifting off the bed. It happens all the time: the riverbed is a vortex factory. But most arches are torn apart or smeared out long before they reach the top. When a strong one makes it to the surface, it joins on, and its two legs become a pair of dimples. From then on, it's a tug of war. Friction slowly spreads a whirlpool out. But where surface water converges and sinks, it stretches the whirlpool and spins it back up. And the dimples you notice are only the lucky ones. A dimple is barely steeper than a ripple, so on a breezy day they're still there, just hidden.

4 · Why flat is special 4:44

There's one more reason they can last, and it's about dimensions. In three dimensions, turbulence works by stretching. Pull a spinning tube longer, and it spins faster and thinner, until it frays apart. Right at the surface, water can hardly move up or down, so the flow there is nearly flat. And a flat flow has nothing to stretch. Instead of tearing apart, whirlpools tend to merge into bigger ones. It's part of why storms in Jupiter's thin, flattened atmosphere can merge into giants like the Great Red Spot. Take that flatness all the way, as a model, and the whirlpools obey Hamilton's rule, the same mathematics as a swinging pendulum. The river's surface becomes its own phase space.

5 · Reading the river 5:46

But real rivers aren't perfectly flat underneath. Water wells up and sinks, and that's where it gets interesting. A team in Trondheim, Norway, led by Omer Babiker and Simen Ellingsen, found that the number of dimples on a patch of water tracks how much its surface is spreading and converging: first in simulations, and this year in a laboratory tank. And that churning helps set how fast a river breathes, trading oxygen and carbon dioxide with the air. So the dimples beside the boat were the ends of vortex lines, half smoke rings risen from the riverbed. Watch one long enough, and you're reading the river.
