Cold open 0:00

On a quiet stretch of the Rhine, we kept seeing them: small dimples on the water, drifting beside the boat. Some lasted a surprisingly long time. Each one marks a whirlpool. Spinning water needs an inward push, and pressure provides it: higher outside, lower toward the centre. So above the low-pressure core, the surface dips. But a river is restless. Everything in it gets sheared and stirred. So what keeps one tiny whirlpool going?

1 · Under the dimple 0:43

Underneath is a narrow tube of spinning water, reaching down into the river. Draw a line along its middle: a vortex line. The water turns around it; the line is how we draw the spin. In 1858, Hermann von Helmholtz described a rule: an isolated tube of spin cannot simply have a loose end inside the water. Draw a loop around it. Along each little piece, take the water's speed in that direction, times the piece's length. Add those contributions: circulation. Freeze the flow. Imagine the tube ended, and slide our measuring loop past that end, without crossing any spinning water. Its circulation should stay the same. But now we can shrink the loop to a point: zero circulation. That contradiction rules out the loose end. A closed ring has no loose end. A tube can also connect to the surface.

2 · Smoke rings 2:01

A smoke ring is that closed shape: a tube of spinning air. The smoke lets us see it roll. In 1867, in Edinburgh, Peter Guthrie Tait showed his smoke rings to his friend William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin. The rings bounced and wobbled without falling apart. Under the right conditions, two rings can even take turns passing through each other. Kelvin saw a possible answer to a much bigger question. In a perfect, frictionless fluid, a ring's circulation would never fade. So maybe atoms were vortex rings in the ether, and each element a different knot. Kelvin was wrong about atoms. But Tait's attempts to catalogue the knots helped build knot theory. Now bring a tilted ring up under the water's surface. Its upper edge arrives first, and it can reconnect, leaving a curved tube with two ends at the surface. From above, two dimples. From below, one connected tube. Its spin points up at one end and down at the other. From above, the ends turn opposite ways.

3 · The river's own smoke rings 3:34

The Rhine doesn't need a smoke box. The current makes spinning structures for itself. One source is the riverbed. Friction slows the water nearest the bottom, while the water above keeps moving. That shear feeds vortices, including arches called hairpins. These structures can be stretched and dispersed. Some can reach the surface and connect to it. In our ideal pair, each whirlpool rides the other's swirl. Bring them closer, keeping their strengths fixed, and the pair moves faster. Obstacles make wakes too. Under suitable conditions, a rock or a bridge pier can shed alternating whirlpools downstream. In this photograph of a Norwegian river, the researchers identify the largest dimples as vortices shed by a bridge pillar. Experiments show that changing the depth over an obstacle can change its wake dramatically.

4 · What keeps one going 4:47

So what keeps a whirlpool going? Left alone, friction spreads its spin into the water around it. The core widens, and the dimple gets shallower. But the river can also work the other way. Where surface water converges and sinks, the downward flow stretches an upright whirlpool and spins it faster. The surrounding surface can stay nearly level. That doesn't mean the water underneath only moves sideways. And a dimple can vanish from view before its whirlpool does. Ripples can hide the dimple without destroying the whirlpool underneath. A shallow dip bends the reflection above it. Keep the whirlpool unchanged, but move a cloud's reflection across it. Now the dimple shows. Smooth water, distinct reflections, and a low viewing angle help. That day, we had all three.

5 · Reading the river 5:56

Those hidden motions are what researchers are learning to read from the surface. A team in Trondheim, including Omer Babiker and Simen Ellingsen, found in simulations that regional dimple counts tracked how strongly the surface was spreading and converging. In 2026, laboratory experiments supported the link, this time measuring the area covered by dimples and by elongated marks called scars. That churning renews the surface, and helps set how fast gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide cross between water and air. We couldn't find a published count of them on any real river. On a sunny day, look from a bridge into clear, shallow water. A dimple can bend sunlight into a dark patch with a bright rim. A dimple isn't a map of everything underneath. But those little dents are clues to the river below.
