Narration as recorded

Cold open 0:00

On a quiet stretch of the Rhine, we kept seeing them: small dimples on the water, drifting beside the boat. Some lasted a surprisingly long time. Each one is the top of a whirlpool. Spinning water needs an inward push, and pressure provides it: higher outside, lower toward the centre. So above the low-pressure core, the surface dips. But a river is restless. Everything in it gets sheared and stirred. So what keeps one tiny whirlpool going?

1 · Under the dimple 0:41

Dive under one, and you find a narrow tube of spinning water, reaching down into the river. Draw a line along its middle, and you have a vortex line. The water turns around it; the line is just how we draw the spin. A line meets the surface in a single point. That point is the dimple. In 1858, Hermann von Helmholtz found a strange rule: a tube of spin can't just have a loose end inside the water. To see why, measure the spin with a loop. Go around it, adding up how fast the water flows along each little piece. That total is the circulation. Now freeze the flow, and slide the loop along the tube and past its end, never crossing any spinning water. The circulation can't change. But past the end, the loop can shrink to nothing, and nothing has no circulation. So the tube can't just stop. It has to close into a ring, or end on something: the riverbed, or the surface.

2 · Smoke rings 2:06

A smoke ring is that ring: a tube of spinning air, closed on itself. The smoke lets you see it roll. In 1867, in Edinburgh, Peter Guthrie Tait showed his smoke rings to his friend William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin. The rings bounced and wobbled without falling apart. Two rings travelling together can even take turns passing through each other. Kelvin saw an answer to a much bigger question. In a perfect, frictionless fluid, a ring's circulation would never fade. So maybe atoms were vortex rings in the ether, and each element a different knot. Kelvin was wrong about atoms. But Tait's attempts to catalogue the knots helped start knot theory. Now bring a ring up under the water's surface. Tilted, its upper edge arrives first, and it can reconnect to the surface, leaving a curved tube with two ends. From above, two dimples. From below, one connected tube. Its spin points up at one end and down at the other, so from above, the two dimples turn opposite ways.

3 · The river's own smoke rings 3:44

The Rhine doesn't need a smoke box. The current makes spinning structures for itself. One source is the riverbed. Friction slows the water nearest the bottom, while the water above races past, and that shear rolls up into arches, like half smoke rings. It happens all the time: the riverbed is a vortex factory. Most arches are torn apart long before they reach the top. But some rise and connect to the surface, leaving a pair of dimples, turning opposite ways. Each rides the other's swirl, carrying the pair along. Bring them closer, and they go faster.

4 · What keeps one going 4:34

So what keeps a whirlpool going? Left alone, friction spreads its spin into the water around it. The core widens, and the dimple gets shallower. But the river can also work the other way. Where surface water converges and sinks, the downward flow stretches an upright whirlpool and spins it faster. The surface can look perfectly flat while this happens. Flat-looking water isn't the same as water moving only sideways. So turbulence doesn't only tear whirlpools down. It can also keep them going. And a dimple can vanish from view before its whirlpool does. Ripples can hide a dimple without destroying the whirlpool underneath.

5 · Reading the river 5:31

Those hidden motions are exactly what researchers are learning to read from the surface. A team in Trondheim, Norway, including Omer Babiker and Simen Ellingsen, found in simulations that the number of dimples tracked how strongly the surface was spreading and converging. This year, laboratory experiments backed up the link, this time measuring the area covered by dimples and by longer marks called scars. That churning renews the surface, and it helps set how fast gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide cross between water and air. A dimple isn't a map of everything underneath. But watch one long enough, and you're reading a little of the river below.
