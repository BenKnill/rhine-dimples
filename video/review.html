Treatment v4: flat water remembers

A story episode, more Veritasium than 3Blue1Brown. The thread is the friend's question: in a turbulent river, how does a whirlpool the size of a coin outlive the chaos that made it? The answer passes through the people and ideas of natural flows, and it has one unifying idea: gravity, layering and rotation flatten natural flows, and flattened flows remember their vortices. The dimples come back at the end as a live research topic: scientists now read what's under a river from its dimples.

Length: about 10 minutes (~1,250 words). Knobs become dial-able experiments where they help, not in every beat. Our own renders throughout (river, pool, rotating tank, soap film, Jupiter-style band); scientists shown as typographic name cards with years (public-domain portraits optional for the 19th-century names).

Cold open (0:00–0:45)~90 words · drafted

  1. From a long wooden boat on the Rhine, a friend kept noticing small dimples on the water, beside the boat and ahead of it. Some lasted a surprisingly long time.
  2. That's strange. A river is turbulent; its eddies should be torn apart within seconds. So how does a whirlpool the size of a coin outlive the chaos that made it?
  3. The answer runs through a Victorian towing tank, a box of smoke rings that convinced Lord Kelvin atoms were knots, a storm on Jupiter that has lasted for nearly two centuries, and a Nobel laureate who showed that whirlpools can have a negative temperature.
Picture: the boat view, dimples surfacing and drifting; quick flashes of each teased image; title.

1 · The numbers that rule natural flows (0:45–2:30)~220 words

Names: Osborne Reynolds (1883, dye streak in a glass pipe: smooth to turbulent); William Froude (towing tank at Torquay, 1870s: scale-model ships make the right waves only if speed over √(g × length) matches).
Concepts: Reynolds number (inertia vs friction): rivers ~10⁶–10⁷, always turbulent. Froude number (speed vs gravity waves): low Froude means gravity wins and the surface lies nearly flat, like a lid. The Rhine beside the boat: turbulent but flat. The same "flattening" appears in the ocean and atmosphere as layering (stratification) and rotation (the Taylor–Proudman effect: G. I. Taylor's rotating-tank columns, 1920s). Most of nature is turbulent and flattened at once.
Picture / knobs: dye in a pipe as Re rises; water over a submerged rock with a live Froude knob: a faint dip at low Fr, a standing wave and hydraulic jump past Fr = 1; a rotating tank where a column of water moves as one.

2 · What flatness does to turbulence (2:30–4:30)~250 words

Names: Lewis Fry Richardson (1922: "Big whorls have little whorls..."); Kolmogorov (1941, the cascade); Fjørtoft (1953), Kraichnan (1967) and Batchelor (1969): in two dimensions the cascade runs backwards; Lars Onsager (1949: point vortices at negative temperature cluster into giants; seen in superfluid experiments in 2019).
Concepts: in 3D, stretching spins eddies up and shreds them small (knob: stretch ×3 → spin ×3); in flat flow nothing stretches, so small eddies merge into big ones. Examples: Jupiter's Great Red Spot (watched since at least 1831), ocean eddies that live for months, "meddies" of salty Mediterranean water that last years, soap films as the lab's 2D fluid (a nod to the friend's other interest and a teaser for episode 4).
Picture: a live 2D turbulence simulation where many small vortices merge into a few big ones; the same seen as a Jupiter-like band; a soap-film tunnel.

3 · The rules of vortices, and the people (4:30–6:30)~250 words

Names: Hermann von Helmholtz (1858: vortex lines can't end in the fluid); P. G. Tait's smoke-ring box and Lord Kelvin's vortex atoms (1867: atoms as knotted vortex rings, eternal by Kelvin's circulation theorem of 1869; wrong about atoms, but Tait's knot tables started knot theory); Gustav Kirchhoff (1876: point vortices obey Hamilton's equations).
Concepts: circulation (loop knob: radius ×2, Γ unchanged); a dimple is where a vortex line meets the surface (dimensionality); the shrinking-loop argument; arches give pairs; pair speed (knob: separation ÷2 → speed ×2); Kirchhoff in one clean step: the river surface is a phase space (brief, with the camel from episode 1).
Picture: smoke rings bouncing and leapfrogging; knotted rings; the 3D tube and surface; the pendulum/whirlpool landscapes.

4 · Where the Rhine's dimples come from (6:30–8:15)~220 words

Names: Gerard Matthes (1947: "kolks" and "boils" in big rivers); Theodore Theodorsen (1952: hairpin vortices); Kline and colleagues at Stanford (1967: hydrogen-bubble films of the wall "bursting"); free-surface turbulence simulations of the 1990s (Perot & Moin; Pan & Banerjee; Shen, Yue and colleagues): vortex ends pinned upright at the surface, which can be fed by sinking water (the Burgers vortex, 1948).
Concepts: the riverbed makes spin; hairpins rise and break the surface as pairs; boils; why they last (the surface end is pinned and mostly left alone, or even spun up); why you see them ahead and beside but not behind; smooth water (ripple knob).
Picture: the hairpin animation; boils in the boat view; the ripple knob.

5 · Reading the river from its dimples (8:15–9:45)~170 words

Names: Simen Ellingsen, Omer Babiker and colleagues (NTNU, 2023 onward): the number of dimples tracks how much water wells up and sinks at the surface (correlation 0.9 in their simulations), and that surface renewal is what controls how fast water exchanges gases with the air (surface-divergence models of gas transfer).
Concepts: dimples as a readout of what's underneath; a step toward measuring how rivers breathe from video of their surface. Close on the boat: the friend's observation was, in effect, a measurement.
Picture: the boat view with dimples counted; a heat-map of upwelling under the surface lining up with the dimples; end card (episode 4: soap films).

Decisions: (1) length about 10 minutes, or split into two episodes (flat water in general; then dimples and their Hamiltonian); (2) how much of the Hamiltonian math stays (proposed: one clean beat in act 3); (3) portraits or name cards only.
